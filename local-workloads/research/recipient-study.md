# People worth backing: thirty grant recipients and a better case for Dharmic Data

The stronger proposal is support for Jai Bhagat’s continuing work as a teacher and builder: making AI feel usable, personal and open to ordinary people’s judgment. A small local studio and a series of public teaching experiments can make that work tangible. A generic workshop budget cannot explain why this person should be backed.

This is a purposive study of thirty distinct recipients: fifteen Emergent Ventures recipients, ten O’Shaughnessy Grants recipients and five O’Shaughnessy Fellows. The programs and award classes are kept separate. The sample favors work adjacent to teaching, creative practice, human agency and practical technology, with scientific projects included to test whether the proposed pattern is merely an education-sector story. It is not a census, an estimate of acceptance probability, or evidence that copying a recipient’s project improves an application.

The announcements establish that these people received support and describe what they were pursuing. They do not disclose all winning applications, evaluators’ private reasons or subsequent results. The table therefore says “announced work,” not “proven impact.” Medical and technical ambitions in promotional profiles should not be mistaken for validated scientific findings.

## What the sample changes about the proposal

A person has a continuing concern before they have an application. Across this sample, the work has a recognizable subject: beauty, conversation, a language, a city, a neglected audience, a research question. My interpretation is that Jai’s subject should be the human experience of meeting powerful technology. “An AI workshop” describes a delivery format; it does not reveal the concern that will keep him working when the novelty wears off.

There is a useful tension between craft and ambition. A film, a journal, a software tool or a teaching practice can be concrete enough to inspect while carrying a larger idea. Jai need not squeeze every ambition into the first funded deliverable. He needs one object that makes the larger ambition believable, followed by a credible route for other people to benefit.

The closest parallels are not all AI researchers. Miller’s teaching, Hamuhuma’s inclusion work and Griffin’s writing make the human audience legible; Baradari’s device and Hariharan’s collaboration work keep human control in view. These are analytical comparisons, not assertions that the funders selected them for the same reason. The lesson is to describe whom Jai wants to serve and what he wants them to be able to do, before discussing inference engines or model compression.

Do not borrow a recipient’s scale, background or authority. A fellowship announcement about a mature scientific project cannot supply evidence for a new educational approach. Likewise, a somatic-literacy recipient does not validate health claims for Jai’s teaching. The comparison can sharpen the question; the proof has to come from Jai’s own work.

## What the funders actually ask for

Emergent Ventures’ current form puts the applicant’s story first, then asks for a mainstream view the applicant agrees with, then the idea and its novelty. It requests a proposal of at most 1,500 words, a self-contained explanation and basic budget information where available. It does not publish a typical award size. The form is accessible; no application was submitted.[^EVFORM]

Cowen’s older explanation of rejection is useful context rather than a current scoring rubric: an idea can be interesting but insufficiently concrete, or enter a field already receiving abundant capital. For Jai, the hard question is what his work adds to a crowded supply of AI tutorials and note-taking products.[^EVWHY]

OSV describes support for individuals and sustained work; its guidance values concise explanations, demonstrable impact and accessible evidence. It permits useful AI assistance but explicitly rejects generic, low-effort AI answers. That is directly relevant to the rejected draft: polished compliance language did not make a case for a person.[^OSVTIPS]

The public OSV FAQ listed April 30, 2026 as the deadline. That date has passed as of this study. The linked 2026 application now presents a password page, so its current questions could not be inspected. A future application window is unverified. Treat OSV material here as preparation, not an open submission opportunity. The FAQ distinguishes Grants from Fellowships and says recipients retain their work’s IP; a venture-backed startup has a different route.[^OSVFAQ][^OSV2026]

## Why the earlier draft failed

It began with a hypothetical pilot rather than a person. It described administrative correctness—eligibility, bounded tasks, evidence, proposed expenses—without showing why Jai cares. It repeatedly defended against invented facts inside the prose, making uncertainty the subject of the application. The result could have belonged to thousands of applicants.

It also optimized for the wrong test. Character limits and arithmetic are necessary submission checks, but passing them says little about whether a reader wants this work to exist. The lecture draft repeated the same mistake: it produced a tidy artifact when the actual need was a place for Jai to collect material, make associations and develop his own teaching.

The revised process separates three layers. First, Jai’s record and stated intent supply the voice and purpose. Second, the research supplies funder-specific questions and comparisons. Third, code checks factual boundaries and form requirements. A generic local model must not invent the first layer to fill gaps in the second.

## The proposed project

The working idea is a personal teaching studio for the encounter with AI. Jai uses it first. It holds the passages, images, questions and observations he wants to think with, and lets him arrange them on a canvas. A local assistant can suggest an association or ask a useful question, but a suggestion does not silently become Jai’s words. The machine supports his preparation; it does not decide what courage means for his audience.

That studio is one tool inside a larger practice: build something, explain why it matters, teach with it, and allow another person’s experience to change the next version. The subject of the initial public teaching work is courage, passion and creative imagination. These are editorial and educational aims; no clinical benefit is claimed.

The first beneficiary is concrete: an independent teacher preparing work from their own sources. Community educators, facilitators and small cultural organizations are plausible later users. They are buyer hypotheses, not a proven market. A platform business would need evidence that another operator can install the tool, trust its data handling and find the resulting preparation worth paying for. Grant support would fund the public learning and reusable method, not prove that business case by itself.

## What would make the case credible

The strongest evidence bundle is small. It includes a working local app, a source-to-lecture example actually used by Jai, a short account of what a learner or collaborator found useful, and a failure whose correction is visible. Technical traces explain how an output was made; they do not replace a person saying the work mattered.

A proposed six-week development period could move through three gates: make the studio useful in Jai’s preparation; use it for one lecture with actual human review; then ask another educator to attempt a similar job. The period is a planning assumption, not a promised calendar. Expansion depends on the outcome of those gates.

| Evidence | What it establishes | What it cannot establish |
|---|---|---|
| Saved source card and canvas reopened after restart | The tool preserves the work | The work is worth doing |
| Optional Bonsai suggestion with an accept/edit trail | Human control and inspectable assistance | Better teaching or better judgment |
| A lecture Jai actually delivers using the material | Use in a real task | A causal learning improvement |
| Another educator completes their own preparation | Early transfer beyond the builder | Broad market demand |
| A specific willingness-to-pay conversation | A buyer signal | A repeatable sales motion |

## Budget without invented numbers

The new ask should purchase focused time and a defined body of work, with actual support costs. Use Jai’s required living/work support for the proposed period, real accessibility or transcription costs, and collaborator payments where people agree to participate. The GB10 already exists, so do not ask for a fictional hardware purchase. Separate funds supporting public work from paid services and explain any expected revenue.

The worksheet accompanying the proposal leaves the total unset until those real costs are entered. An ungrounded round number would weaken the proposal even if it happened to match an award in this sample. For EV, project-specific cost is the appropriate starting point. For OSV, the distinction between a small Grant and a year-long Fellowship should follow actual commitment and scope.

## Editorial review before submission

The applicant should recognize the draft as their own intention. The most valuable missing addition is one true encounter from Jai’s teaching: what someone wanted to do, what stood in their way, and what changed when they tried. That encounter is not available in the source packet and has not been invented. It can replace a general paragraph when Jai supplies it.

The mainstream-belief answer is also a personal judgment; the supplied option is explicitly proposed for review. Dates, time commitment and the ask need confirmation. These are few, specific gaps kept in a preparation checklist rather than repeated as disclaimers inside every sentence of the proposal.
